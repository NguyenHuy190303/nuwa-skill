## 1. An overview of his books

### Trump's major published books

| Year | Title | Co-author | Nature |
|------|------|--------|------|
| 1987 | *Trump: The Art of the Deal* | Tony Schwartz (ghostwriter) | a business memoir + a negotiation philosophy |
| 1990 | *Trump: Surviving at the Top* | Charles Leerhsen | a sequel |
| 1997 | *Trump: The Art of the Comeback* | Kate Bohner | a retrospective on rebuilding after bankruptcy |
| 2004 | *Trump: How to Get Rich* | Meredith McIver | business advice |
| 2004 | *Trump: Think Like a Billionaire* | Meredith McIver | business advice |
| 2006 | *Trump 101: The Way to Success* | Meredith McIver | self-help |
| 2007 | *Think Big and Kick Ass in Business and Life* | Bill Zanker | motivation/drive |
| 2008 | *Why We Want You to Be Rich* | Robert Kiyosaki | co-authored wealth education |
| 2015 | *Crippled America: How to Make America Great Again* | sole author | a campaign political manifesto |

**An important reliability caveat**: *The Art of the Deal* was actually written by ghostwriter
Tony Schwartz. Schwartz has stated: "Trump didn't write a postcard for us." (source: publisher
Howard Kaminsky.) Trump himself has been inconsistent — first saying "I chose Schwartz to
write the book," later saying "Schwartz didn't write it, I did." ★★★ (a primary source:
Schwartz's interviews with The New Yorker/NPR, 2016)

So the principles in *The Art of the Deal* reflect **Schwartz's observations of Trump**,
rather than something written by Trump's own hand — but the stories and business logic in the
book have been confirmed by Trump himself, and represent his public self-narrative.

## 2. *The Art of the Deal* (1987): the 11 negotiation principles

> Source: the principle summary following Chapter 1, "Dealing: A Week in the Life of Donald
> Trump"
> Reliability: ★★ (ghostwritten by Schwartz, but the principles are endorsed by Trump and
> repeatedly cited throughout his political career)

### The full 11 principles, unpacked

**1. Think Big**
Don't set small goals. Trump believes most people limit their own ambitions out of fear of big
decisions — fundamentally, fear of success. He describes his own drive as "controlled
neurosis" — an obsessive focus on the direction of his work.
> Original: "I like thinking big. I always have. To me it's very simple: if you're going to be
> thinking anyway, you might as well think big."

**2. Protect the Downside / Prepare for the Worst**
Success isn't a gamble — it's structurally ensuring failure can't be fatal. He always keeps an
exit, demands guarantees, and sets exit clauses in negotiation. Advance incrementally, avoid
catastrophic loss.
> Logic: most deals will fail, so make sure every failure is survivable.

**3. Maintain Flexibility / Have a Backup Plan**
Never bet on just one hand. Always keep Plan B, C, D running, and don't get too attached to
any single outcome. Trump pivoted to convention-center development after his New York
residential projects hit obstacles, validating this principle.

**4. Know Your Market / Know Your Audience**
Doesn't rely on market research and consultant reports — relies on **intuition and
intelligence gathered directly from real people**. "I trust my own instincts more than any
survey." His grasp of audience psychology is instinctive, not data-driven.

**5. Use Leverage**
Never appear desperate. Position yourself as the solution to someone else's problem, not as
someone in need.
> Original (from the book): "My style of deal-making is quite simple and straightforward. I
> aim very high, and then I just keep pushing and pushing and pushing to get what I'm after."

**6. Enhance Location Through Deal Structure**
Challenges the conventional real-estate rule of "location, location, location." The key isn't
finding a perfect location — it's manufacturing value in an ordinary location through creative
deal structure. Marketing and perception can change reality.

**7. Leverage Media / Promote**
Free media coverage is cheaper and more credible than advertising. The core concept: **"truthful
hyperbole"** — harmless exaggeration, captures attention, drives business.
> Original: "I play to people's fantasies. People may not always think big themselves, but
> they can still get very excited by those who do... I call it truthful hyperbole. It's an
> innocent form of exaggeration—and it's a very effective form of promotion."

**8. Fight Back If You Think You're Right**
Success invites envy and attack. Hold your ground, even at the cost of making enemies. Better
to fight and have regrets than give up — even if you lose, at least you know you used every
option.

**9. Deliver Real Value / Have a Good Product**
Marketing can't substitute for substance forever. It can carry you short term; long term, you
need to deliver for real. Building credibility depends on **keeping your promises**.

**10. Keep Costs Low / Contain Costs**
Even on a multi-million-dollar project, negotiate every dollar. It adds up — control spending
strictly.

**11. Enjoy the Ride / Have Fun**
Negotiation itself is a kind of game — enjoying the process matters more than fixating on the
material outcome. Stay present, don't dwell on the past or future.

### A paradox about negotiation

The book contains a self-contradiction: Trump writes that offering negotiation tips at all
runs against one of his own principles — "great negotiators are born, not made." He believes
negotiation is mostly instinct, not a learnable technique. ★★★

## 3. His definition of, and obsession with, "winning"

> Source: across multiple books + speeches + biographical research
> Reliability: ★★★ (direct quotes) + ★★ (biographical research)

### Trump's core definition of "winning"

**Winning is personal validation, not just a material outcome**

His campaign slogan: "We're going to win so much, you're going to be so sick and tired of
winning." (a 2016 campaign rally) ★★★

In his political narrative, "winning" and "losing" are the basic frame for understanding
America's position:
- "America doesn't win anymore. We lose to China on trade, to our enemies militarily, and to
  every country on diplomacy." (recurring in campaign speeches) ★★★
- "Success shouldn't be measured only by money, but by happiness too." (*The Art of the
  Comeback*, 1997) ★★★

### A psychological perspective (secondary)

Biographer Michael D'Antonio (*Never Enough*, 2015) argues Trump's trademark approach is built
on the firm belief that "he is an unusual, superior human being." Trump has admitted he avoids
self-reflection "because I might not like what I see," and believes "most people don't deserve
respect." ★★

A psychological analysis introduces the concept of "the episodic man" — Trump lacks a coherent
life narrative, living episode by episode, needing to win whatever battle is in front of him
right now. "For Trump, truth is whatever helps win in the present moment." ★ (a secondary
inference)

### His father's influence

Fred Trump's parenting style: extremely transactional — success was rewarded, failure ignored
or punished. He reportedly told Donald: "you are a king, but a king has to work harder than
anyone to prove he deserves it." ★★ (from the D'Antonio biography)

### The "winning" theme repeated across his books (3+ occurrences)

| Theme | Where it recurs |
|------|-------------|
| America is losing on trade | *Crippled America*, 2016 speeches, the inaugural address |
| We elected stupid negotiators | *Crippled America*, campaign rallies, Twitter |
| Winning requires toughness, the weak get exploited | *The Art of the Deal*, *The Art of the Comeback*, political speeches |
| Retaliation is a necessary part of success | *Think Big and Kick Ass*, *The Art of the Comeback* |

## 4. His theory of media and information

> Source: *The Art of the Deal* (1987), *Think Big and Kick Ass* (2007), campaign speeches
> Reliability: ★★★ (quoted directly from his books)

### 4.1 "Truthful hyperbole" theory

This is **one of the very few concepts Trump named himself** in The Art of the Deal:

> "I call it truthful hyperbole. It's an innocent form of exaggeration—and it's a very
> effective form of promotion."

The logic: people want to believe something is the biggest, the best, the most spectacular. If
you give them that fantasy, you're serving them. It's not a lie — it's "harmless
exaggeration."

### 4.2 Media as a tool that can be played

Trump has spoken publicly many times about how he "plays the press":
- media needs a good story, and you can make the story better with exaggeration and
  controversy
- even bad publicity brings attention, and the key is turning it into a business
  opportunity
- when asked how he stays famous without spending on advertising, Trump answered: "you make
  news."

A key observation: "The press takes him literally, but not seriously; his supporters take him
seriously, but not literally." This distinction reveals how "truthful hyperbole" lands
differently on different audiences in the political context. ★★

### 4.3 The politicization of "fake news" (post-2016)

Across his political career, Trump turned "fake news" from a descriptive term into an
**offensive weapon**:
- originally used to criticize media coverage that misreported him
- gradually expanded to label any media coverage critical of him
- its actual function: **preemptively destroying the credibility of any information source
  unfavorable to him**

This is a theoretical continuation of the media strategy in The Art of the Deal: control the
narrative, don't let others define you.

### 4.4 A recorded contradiction

In 1987, Trump actively maintained relationships with reporters and heavily used the media;
but during his political career, he came to describe the media as "the enemy of the people."

Possible explanations (recorded, not reconciled):
- Path A: he's always been "using" the media — the stance itself is a strategy
- Path B: the media was his ally in 1987 and became his adversary after 2016, with his
  position flipping as his interests did

## 5. The underlying logic of America First

> Source: *Crippled America* (2015), his April 2016 foreign-policy speech, his 2017 inaugural
> address (official archive)
> Reliability: ★★★ (primary sources)

### 5.1 The core argument structure

Trump's "America First" isn't isolationism — it's **the globalization of business-negotiation
thinking**:

1. **Diagnosis**: America is being run by bad negotiators who've signed a series of terrible
   deals
2. **Attribution**: career politicians, special-interest groups, globalist elites
3. **Solution**: replace diplomatic convention with business-negotiation principles — tough
   demands, mutual-benefit exchanges, no paying for values

His inaugural address, quoted directly: "a small group in our nation's Capital has reaped the
rewards of government while the people have borne the cost." ★★★

### 5.2 A zero-sum view of trade

Trump's trade philosophy is standard **zero-sum game theory**: a trade deficit = America is
losing; another country selling more and buying less = America being taken advantage of;
tariffs = a tit-for-tat weapon.

A direct quote (2024): "To me, the most beautiful word in the dictionary is tariff, and it's
my favourite word." ★★★

His historical claim (recurring): "From 1789 to 1913 we were a tariff-backed nation, and the
United States was proportionately the wealthiest it has ever been." ★★★
(Note: this is his own historical interpretation; economists assess it differently — recorded
here only as his argument)

### 5.3 A national narrative frame

His inaugural address's three-layer narrative:
- globalization "redistributed" wealth from the American middle class to the rest of the world
- "the forgotten men and women" are the moral basis for policy reform
- "This American carnage stops right here and stops right now" (his characterization of
  America's state at the time) ★★★

This narrative directly grafts onto The Art of the Deal's concept of "leverage": America has
lost its negotiating leverage, and a tough leader needs to take it back.

### 5.4 National interest before values

His April 2016 foreign-policy speech (a primary text):

> "No country has ever prospered that failed to put its own interests first. We will no longer
> surrender this country or its people to the false song of globalism. The nation-state
> remains the true foundation for happiness and harmony." ★★★

Academic analysis distills five features of Trumpist foreign policy (★★, secondary):
- prefers bilateral over multilateral arrangements
- pursues near-term economic gains over durable commitments
- a zero-sum view of international relations
- indifference to a partner nation's political system
- no macro-strategic vision

## 6. Transferring business thinking to political decisions

> Source: *Crippled America* (2015), academic analysis literature
> Reliability: ★★ (synthesized analysis)

### 6.1 Trump's self-positioning: the businessman president

The core argument of *Crippled America*:
- career politicians fail because they treat politics as politics
- America's problems are fundamentally management problems
- a successful business negotiator can solve what government can't
- being an "outsider" is an asset, not a liability

The logic in *Crippled America*: "no business of any size should pay more than 15% in
taxes" — treating tax reform as a corporate pricing problem. ★★★ (quoted directly from the
book)

### 6.2 Business principles transferred directly

| Business principle (from The Art of the Deal) | Political application |
|----------------------------------|---------|
| Open high, leave room | Tariff negotiations: open at an extreme high, then "concede" down to the target level |
| Use leverage, never look desperate | Diplomatic negotiations: threaten to leave an alliance, renegotiate terms |
| Always counterattack | Attacking and retaliating against critics |
| Any publicity is good publicity | Controversial statements as a media strategy |
| Control the narrative | The "fake news" label preemptively undermines an opponent's credibility |

### 6.3 The fundamental tension between business logic and political logic (recording the
contradiction)

Scholars note a fundamental difference:
- a business deal can be walked away from; a country can't truly exit geopolitics
- a business rival can be swapped out; an alliance relationship carries historical trust costs
- once a deal closes, the relationship can be settled; international relations are an ongoing
  game

Trump's business logic is effective in some settings (bilateral trade negotiation) and
produces systemic friction in others (multilateral alliances, values-based diplomacy). ★

## 8. Recommended reading and intellectual lineage

> Source: books Trump has mentioned in his writing and interviews
> Reliability: ★★ (public statements; how much he actually read is hard to verify)

### Books Trump says he's read/recommends

- *The Art of the Deal* / *Surviving at the Top* (his own books, listed as favorites)
- *The Prince* by Machiavelli — recommended in *Trump 101*
- *The Art of War* by Sun Tzu — recommended in *Trump 101*
- The collected essays of Ralph Waldo Emerson — reportedly a favorite in his early years
- *All Quiet on the Western Front* — calls it "one of the greatest books ever"
- Several conservative political books attacking Obama and Hillary Clinton (written by Fox
  News hosts and others)

An observation: the books Trump recommends are heavily books written about himself, books
criticizing his opponents, and classical works that emphasize power and victory (Machiavelli,
Sun Tzu). ★★

### His core intellectual lineage: two key figures

**Norman Vincent Peale (author of The Power of Positive Thinking)**
- Trump attended services at Peale's Marble Collegiate Church from a young age
- Peale's core doctrine: only think about the best outcome; "accentuate the positive,
  eliminate the negative"
- Trump has called himself Peale's "greatest student ever" (not independently verified) ★
- Influence on Trump: maintaining confidence through adversity; reframing real problems as
  winnable challenges
- Source: CNBC (2020), several biographical studies ★★★

**Roy Cohn (a lawyer, an advisor to the McCarthy-era investigative committee)**
- Trump's lawyer and mentor (1973-1986)
- Cohn's style: never compromise, counterattack every criticism, destroy the opponent
- Biographers argue: never admit fault, always counterattack — this traces directly to Cohn
- Source: biographical research ★★

## 10. Documented contradictions and inconsistencies

> Reliability: ★★★ (with textual evidence)

| Domain | Position A | Position B |
|------|-------|-------|
| The Iraq War | supported it in pre-2003 interviews | claimed during his campaign that he'd always opposed it |
| Syrian refugees | one 2015 interview: humanitarian considerations mean accepting them | the very next day: can't accept them |
| Abortion | described himself as pro-choice in earlier years | supported restrictions after taking office |
| Attitude toward the media | 1987: actively used the media, maintained relationships | post-2016: "the enemy of the people" |
| Debt | used extensive leveraged borrowing in his own businesses | criticizes national debt politically |
| Authorship of The Art of the Deal | "I chose Schwartz to write it" | "Schwartz didn't write it, I did" |
| Trade protectionism | used foreign steel in his own businesses (documented) | policy demands using American steel |

**Markers of a stable "real belief"** (recurring 3+ times across time, unchanged by shifting
interests):
- America has been mismanaged by bad negotiators
- a trade deficit is proof of losing
- the strong are respected, the weak are exploited
- fight back, never show weakness
