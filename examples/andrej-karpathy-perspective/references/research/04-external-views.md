## 1. Assessments from peers and colleagues

### Sam Altman

- When Karpathy returned to OpenAI for the second time (February 2023), Altman tweeted
  "@karpathy welcome back!" ★★★
- **A core disagreement**: Altman predicts AI will exceed human intelligence in any
  professional domain by 2030; Karpathy calls his own AGI timeline "5 to 10 times more
  pessimistic than the mainstream forecast." Karpathy has directly rebutted the optimistic
  predictions of Altman, Dario Amodei, and Jensen Huang in public, saying they "overstate AI's
  capability." ★★★ (Fortune reporting, October 2025)

### Ilya Sutskever

- The two left OpenAI at nearly the same time (Karpathy in February 2024, Sutskever in June
  2024), but for completely different reasons and in completely different directions.
- Sutskever took part in the November 2023 board coup against Altman; Karpathy maintained a
  friendly relationship with OpenAI throughout, a clear divergence from Sutskever's path.
- An outside observer: "the two of them respectively represent two fundamentally different
  visions — AI as your mentor (Karpathy) and AI as your god (Sutskever)." ★★

### Andrew Ng

Both are central figures in deep-learning education, but they clashed directly over the
concept of "vibe coding."

Ng, speaking at the LangChain Interrupt event in June 2025: "I regret that this thing is
called vibe coding, because the name misleads people into thinking engineers are just going
with their gut. In reality, guiding AI to write useful software is deep intellectual work. I
spend a whole day doing AI-assisted programming, and frankly, I'm exhausted by the end of it."
★★★

Ng isn't dismissing AI-assisted programming itself — he thinks Karpathy's naming choice
creates a cognitive misunderstanding.

### Richard Sutton (a founder of the reinforcement-learning field)

Karpathy and Sutton have a substantive disagreement over the field's direction. Karpathy
proposes "we're summoning ghosts," pushing back against Sutton's "we're building animals"
framework.

Sutton considers LLMs a "dead end," emphasizing that RL and continual learning are the real
path forward; Karpathy doesn't accept RL as the main path, calling it "sucking supervision
through a straw" — carrying a fundamental noise problem. ★★★

### Fei-Fei Li (his PhD advisor)

The two co-taught Stanford's CS231n, which grew from 150 students in 2015 to 750 in 2017,
indirect evidence of how highly the course is regarded. No direct public statement from Fei-Fei
Li assessing Karpathy has been found. ★★ (indirect evidence)

## 2. Industry reaction to his departures

### Leaving Tesla (July 2022)

The industry reaction was fairly shocked. Fortune's headline: "Who is Andrej Karpathy? Tesla's
AI chief abruptly resigns, and that spells trouble for Elon Musk." ★★★

An outside analysis (Medium): the "real reason" for leaving may have been a long-standing
tension between Musk's overly optimistic public FSD promises and Karpathy's actual engineering
assessment — Karpathy never publicly oversold progress. ★ (speculative analysis, limited
reliability)

### Leaving OpenAI (February 2024)

In Karpathy's own words: "nothing happened, it's not the result of any event or drama."
TechCrunch's headline: "Andrej Karpathy leaves OpenAI again — but he says there's no drama."
★★★

This contrasts with Sutskever's departure at roughly the same time; outside media generally
bundled the two together in coverage, but the actual reasons were completely different:
Karpathy left by his own choice, Sutskever left after a political defeat.

## 3. The controversy over "vibe coding"

### The original definition

"There's a new kind of coding I call vibe coding — you fully give in to the vibes, embrace
exponentials, and forget that the code even exists." ★★★

### Supporters' arguments

Simon Willison (co-creator of Django): highly appreciative of Karpathy's original definition,
calling it "precise and fun," since Karpathy is a top-tier programmer and the term describes a
specific mode of exploration, not an argument for abandoning understanding. ★★★

### Critics' arguments

1. **Andrew Ng's naming critique**: the term itself is misleading, making people think the
   work is "just going with your gut," when AI-assisted programming is actually demanding
   intellectual work. ★★★
2. **Security vulnerability risk**: a December 2025 CodeRabbit analysis found AI-collaborated
   code has a 2.74x higher security-vulnerability rate than human-written code. ★★★
3. **Maintainability problems**: Fast Company reported a "vibe coding hangover" — senior
   engineers describing falling into "development hell" after inheriting an AI-generated
   codebase. ★★★
4. **Beginners' skill atrophy**: critics worry vibe coding eliminates the entry-level tasks
   beginners need to learn programming, breaking the skill pipeline. ★★

### The 2026 reversal

Karpathy himself has declared vibe coding "already dated," with his new preferred term being
"agentic engineering": "by default, you're not spending 99% of your time writing code
directly — you're orchestrating agents and acting as a supervisor." ★★★

## 4. The "job risk map" deletion incident (March 2026)

### What happened

Karpathy "vibe coded" an interactive chart in two hours, scoring 342 BLS occupations on
AI-exposure risk (0-10). The chart showed white-collar occupations scoring highest and manual-
labor occupations scoring lowest. Elon Musk reposted it, commenting "all jobs will be
optional," and it went viral fast.

Within hours, Karpathy deleted the GitHub repo. His explanation: "'Exposure' was scored by an
LLM based on how digitized the job is. It has nothing to do with what will actually happen to
these jobs. People are distorting this visualization and putting words in my mouth." ★★★

### Community criticism

- **A methodological flaw**: using LLM scoring as a proxy for labor-market impact is
  methodologically too crude.
- Some observers read this episode as the cost of Karpathy's "public experimentation culture":
  he's willing to share unfinished ideas publicly, but when media amplification distorts them,
  he tends to retreat rather than defend the debate. ★★

## 5. Outside assessments of Eureka Labs

### Optimism

TechCrunch's coverage is generally positive, treating it as a natural extension: from Stanford
CS231n, to YouTube teaching videos, to a formal startup. ★★★

### Skepticism and criticism (Dan Meyer, a math educator)

Dan Meyer's Substack post, "Andrej Karpathy Is in Trouble," is the most substantial public
critique to date:

- **Precedents of failure**: Sebastian Thrun's Udacity and Andrew Ng's Coursera were both
  pioneers of elite-technologist-led online education, and neither achieved its grand
  educational-transformation goal.
- **A core contradiction**: "very few people designing educational software have experience
  successfully running a classroom or a school." Karpathy helped build some of the world's
  most advanced computing technology, but he now has to pour all his creativity into the much
  harder task of "helping people learn."
- **The historical failure of scaling learning**: "every technology that has promised to scale
  learning has fallen short of its own hype." ★★★

## 6. Assessments from the AI-learner community

### Highly positive (the mainstream view)

- Google Scholar shows over 78,000 citations (as of the research date). ★★★
- The "Zero to Hero" series is widely considered one of the best introductory courses in deep
  learning; DeepLearning.AI lists him among its "Heroes of Deep Learning." ★★★
- His teaching style is highly praised as "authentic": emphasizing "don't abstract anything
  away," coding live and showing bug fixes as they happen.

### Minor criticism (a smaller number of voices)

- Some learners feel the course assumes a fair amount of prior background, making "zero to
  hero" somewhat of an overstatement.
- Hacker News discussion of Eureka Labs: some excited, some taking a "prove it to me" wait-and-
  see stance.

## 8. Behavior patterns observed from outside

### 1. A culture of public experimentation, sometimes ending abruptly

The job-risk-map incident is the textbook case: release something unfinished -> it goes viral
-> delete and clarify. He acts first and thinks later, but when the social consequences exceed
his expectations, he tends to retreat rather than argue it out. ★★★

### 2. Willing to oppose industry consensus

During the AGI-bubble period, he was one of the few top figures willing to say publicly "the
models are not there" and "the product is slop." TradeFox's CEO: "if this Karpathy interview
can't puncture the AI bubble, nothing can." ★★★ (Fortune, October 2025)

### 3. Speaks fast, thinks ahead of his own speech

Karpathy admits this himself: "I know, I speak too fast. It works against me, because
sometimes my speaking thread out-executes my thinking."  ★★★

### 4. An intriguing relationship with Elon Musk

Musk reposted his job risk map, and the two appear to stay in touch, but Karpathy has never
publicly stated support for Musk's political actions. Some analysis reads his departure from
Tesla as reflecting an underlying tension with Musk's culture of "overly optimistic public
promises." ★ (speculative, no direct evidence)

### 5. A low-key personal life, high-profile technical opinions

No credible reporting on his private life has been found. His public and private personas
appear to essentially coincide — technical blog posts, course videos, technical commentary on
X.

## 10. Core distinguishing traits (as observed from outside)

Compared to his generation of AI leaders, outside observers commonly note the following:

1. **A dual scarcity**: he's both a top-tier engineer and a top-tier communicator. LeCun can
   research but communicates in a way that's hard for a general audience; many science
   communicators can explain but lack engineering depth.
2. **Institutional independence**: he's moved between Stanford, Tesla, OpenAI, and Eureka
   Labs, never tied to a single institution, which makes his public statements more credible.
3. **A constructive critic**: he criticizes AI hype without denying AI's value — a sharp
   contrast to the "anti-AI" stance of people like Gary Marcus.
4. **Conceptual productivity**: "Software 2.0" (2017), "vibe coding" (2025), "summoning
   ghosts" (2025), "agentic engineering" (2026) — he regularly contributes terms that
   circulate across the industry.
5. **Public vulnerability**: he's willing to say publicly "I've never felt this much behind as
   a programmer" (2025), and to admit he speaks too fast — rare among top-tier AI leaders.
