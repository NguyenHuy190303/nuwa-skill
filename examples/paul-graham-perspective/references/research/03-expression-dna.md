# Paul Graham - Expression-Style DNA

> Research date: 2026-04-05
> Research goal: extract PG's linguistic fingerprint from Twitter/X, his essays, and his controversial stances, as source material for the expression layer of the skill

---

## 1. Overall style thesis: precision disguised as simplicity

PG's writing style is often summarized as "writing the way you talk," but several analysts point out this is a carefully constructed illusion.

**Core contradiction**: simple on the surface, precise underneath.

- Sasha Chapin's analysis: PG "is not a simple writer. He's complex and subtle. It's just that the complexity isn't at the word level, so you don't notice it unless you look closely." (Source: [Sasha Chapin, Substack](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer))
- Henry Oliver's analysis: PG's style resembles "the realism of theater or film" — it imitates real conversation, but is carefully structured. He assembles "near-perfect rhythm" out of "simple atoms." (Source: [Henry Oliver, Common Reader](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

**Quantifiable traits**:
- About 70% of his essays contain the phrase "for example," meaning an abstract claim is followed by a concrete example within at most 1-2 sentences
- 200+ essays, sustained output spanning 25+ years
- Prefers simple Germanic-root words, but empirical analysis shows his ratio of Latinate words is not significantly different from peers like Sam Altman (Source: [Arjun Panickssery, Substack](https://arjunpanickssery.substack.com/p/germanic-vs-latinate-words-in-writing))

## 2. Sentence templates and high-frequency expressions

### 2.1 Classic sentence templates

| Template | Example | Usage context |
|------|------|---------|
| **"The way to X is to Y"** | "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems." | revealing a counterintuitive method |
| **"Most people don't realize..."** | "Most people dislike uncertainty" | introducing a common blind spot |
| **"It turns out..."** | "It turns out to be very useful to work on what interests you the most" | revealing an unexpected finding |
| **"X is like Y"** | "Startups are as unnatural as skiing" / a programming language "should be a pencil, not a pen" | explanation by analogy |
| **"If you X, you Y"** | "If you're in a job that feels safe, you are not going to get exceptional" | conditional causation |
| **"The [adj]-est [noun] are..."** | "The statements that make people maddest are those they worry might be true" | an extreme-form claim |
| **"There's a [noun] that..."** | "There's a third ingredient that's not as well understood" | introducing an overlooked factor |
| **"I think" / "I suspect"** | "I suspect few housing projects in the US were designed by architects who expected to live in them" | modest hedge + a sharp observation |
| **Parallel "[X] and [Y]"** | "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" | parallel structure creating rhythm |

### 2.2 Opening patterns (essay openings)

PG's openings rarely use a definition or a quotation; instead he uses these patterns:

1. **Personal anecdote**: "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" / "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years"
2. **Common wisdom + turn**: "Everyone knows that to do great work you need both natural ability and determination. But there's a third ingredient that's not as well understood"
3. **A bold claim stated directly**: gets straight to the point, no setup
4. **Antipophora (asking and answering his own question)**: poses a question, then unfolds an exploratory answer

### 2.3 High-frequency vocabulary preferences

**Words he favors**:
- Simple verbs: think, know, want, need, make, start, try, seem, work
- Hedges: probably, usually, most, roughly, fairly, somewhat
- Colloquial connectors: but, so, and, though, because
- Metaphorical words: seed, path, landscape, machine, lever

**Words he explicitly dislikes**:
- "delve" — PG publicly stated on Twitter in 2024 that this is a telltale ChatGPT word, sparking the "Delve-gate" controversy
- "burgeoning" — also flagged by him as a marker of bookish/AI-flavored writing
- Academic jargon (methodology, utilize, facilitate, etc.)
- Corporate buzzwords (synergy, "leverage" as a verb, paradigm shift, etc.)

## 3. Rhetorical toolbox

### 3.1 Parallelism

PG makes heavy use of matched two-part sentence structures to build rhythm:
- Mirrored structures like "had" / "having," "sold" / "suddenly"
- Tricolon (a rule-of-three structure): the last element is deliberately lengthened to add weight, e.g. "natural ability, determination, and an obsessive interest in a particular topic"

(Source: [Henry Oliver's analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

### 3.2 Self-riffing

After introducing a word into a paragraph, PG will "call back" to that same word in later sentences, using the language itself as a bridge for the argument. Sasha Chapin says this makes "moving from one point to the next feel buttery smooth."

(Source: [Sasha Chapin's analysis](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer))

### 3.3 Strategic "elevated vocabulary"

PG generally uses the simplest possible words, but occasionally drops in a precisely placed elevated expression for impact. Chapin cites "spectral signature of a hater" as an example — powerful because of its rarity, producing a triple effect: satisfaction, emphasis of a judgment, and attention-grabbing.

### 3.4 Antipophora (asking and answering his own question)

PG's essays frequently advance an argument through self-posed questions and answers, similar to the structure of a Shakespearean soliloquy. Rather than compressing a question into a single sentence, he walks through each stage of the thought.

### 3.5 "Chattiness"

Specific techniques include:
- Mixing contractions: "I'm" and "they are" appearing in the same paragraph (imitating the inconsistency of real speech)
- Filler emphasis: colloquial intensifiers like "very specific"
- Strings of consecutive questions
- Mixing high and low registers (a highbrow idea + a colloquial expression)

## 4. Twitter/X expression style

### 4.1 Tweet patterns

PG's style on Twitter differs noticeably from his essays — shorter, sharper, more provocative.

**Typical tweet sentence types**:
- **Aphoristic**: "If you optimize for engagement, it turns out you optimize for lies"
- **Analogy-based**: "You don't become a tech hub by being the new home of Amazon, but by being the home of the new Amazon"
- **Observational**: "It's a good sign when people misuse your software to do Y. It means they want Y a lot"
- **Sarcastic**: mocking politicians for using ChatGPT to generate policy — "I doubt we can improve the hardware as fast as you can improve the software" (April 2025, to Sam Altman)
- **Stance-taking**: "The deprioritization of tweets with links in them is Twitter's biggest flaw"

(Source: [Paul Graham @paulg on X](https://x.com/paulg))

### 4.2 Quantified tweet characteristics

- Prefers short tweets of 1-3 sentences
- Frequent use of contrastive structure (A, but B / not X, but Y)
- Rarely uses emoji or hashtags
- Often attaches a shared link (and criticizes the X platform for deprioritizing tweets with links)
- Replies to others tend to be short, direct, and sometimes barbed

## 5. Controversies and response patterns

### 5.1 Major controversies

| Time | Event | PG's stance | Response style |
|------|------|---------|---------|
| April 2024 | "Delve-gate" — called "delve" a telltale ChatGPT word | held his original view | kept arguing, later made some tweets private |
| December 2022 | Suspended from Twitter for posting a Mastodon link | publicly declared he was leaving Twitter | a high-profile statement, later returned |
| 2013 | Controversial remarks about women in tech | said "if you were going to be really good at programming, you'd have found it on your own" | did not substantially retreat |
| 2024 | Shared a Gaza medical report | reposted an account from 65 medical staff in Gaza | reposted directly, with minimal commentary |
| Multiple times | Debate over economic inequality | argued that inequality arising from entrepreneurship is good | wrote long essays to argue it in detail |

(Sources: [Medium analysis](https://medium.com/@moyosoreale/the-paul-graham-vs-nigerian-twitter-saga-lexical-racism-and-language-bias-masked-as-chatgpt-53ee9f6459aa), [Fortune reporting](https://fortune.com/2022/12/18/twitter-suspends-paul-graham-venture-capitalist-supportive-of-elon-musk-after-he-tweets-about-mastodon-link/), [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/issie-lapowsky/paul-graham-women-in-tech.html))

### 5.2 Patterns in responding to criticism

PG himself wrote "How to Disagree," proposing a hierarchy of disagreement levels (DH0-DH6). But how he actually behaves on Twitter:

- **Tends to double down** rather than apologize or back off
- **Hedges with "I think"** while never abandoning the core claim
- **Critics point out**: he sometimes doesn't fully engage with the other side's core argument, and shows a tendency toward straw-manning
- **Debate style**: prefers short, direct counterpunches over lengthy back-and-forth
- **Silence as a strategy**: once a controversy escalates past a certain point, he'll make tweets private or stop responding

## 6. His sense of humor

PG's humor isn't stand-up-style performance — it's the dry wit and irony of a scholar:

### 6.1 Types of humor

| Type | Trait | Example |
|------|------|------|
| **Analogical irony** | uses an unexpected analogy to expose absurdity | "Dressing up is inevitably a substitute for good ideas. It is no coincidence that technically inept business types are known as 'suits'" |
| **Subverted expectation** | states a surprising conclusion in a serious tone | "I wish they had just told us outright that we were savages and our world was stupid" (talking about school bullying) |
| **Self-deprecating modesty** | pokes fun at himself to introduce a point | "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years" |
| **Deadpan statement** | states a radical view in a flat tone | "Startups often have to do dubious things" |
| **Absurdist comparison** | compares two normally unrelated things | comparing Navy SEAL training to American high schoolers' pursuit of popularity |

### 6.2 Principles of his humor

- Humor is never the point itself, but seasoning for the argument
- Never uses the form of a joke or one-liner
- The target of the irony is usually an institution, a system, or a foolish convention, not an individual
- Low density of humor — an essay might have only 2-4 clear moments of it

## 7. Writing process and methodology

### 7.1 PG's self-described writing method

| Dimension | Specific practice | Source |
|------|---------|------|
| **Daily schedule** | from morning drop-off to afternoon pickup, about 5 hours | [Writing Routines interview](https://www.writingroutines.com/paul-graham-interview/) |
| **Working environment** | a quiet room, a door that closes, at least one window | same |
| **Method** | writes a rough draft first, then revises repeatedly, cutting heavily | [Writing, Briefly](https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html) |
| **Revision intensity** | reads every sentence 100+ times before publishing | [How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html) |
| **Breaking through a block** | walking. Sometimes pacing in the office is enough, sometimes he needs to go outside | Writing Routines interview |
| **His quality test** | whether the writing process surprises him | same |
| **Feedback loop** | has friends read drafts; has killed at least two complete essays outright | same |
| **Reference on speed** | the essay "Writing, Briefly" took 67 minutes (23 minutes writing, 44 minutes revising) | Writing, Briefly |
| **Draft vs. final** | 80% of an essay's ideas only emerge during the writing process | Writing, Briefly |
| **Unpublished practice** | wrote essays in a notebook for 15 years without publishing them, as a thinking tool | How to Write Usefully |

### 7.2 PG's four standards for writing

A good essay = **importance × novelty × correctness × strength** (all four multiplied together).

- **Correctness**: claims should be as strong as possible without crossing into fallacy. He uses Pike's Peak as an analogy — saying it's "near the middle of Colorado" is more useful than "somewhere in Colorado," but saying it's "in the exact center" would be wrong
- **Strength**: bold, but true — avoiding both academic hedging-into-vagueness and a demagogue's false certainty
- **The legitimate use of hedges**: "I think" isn't weakness — it's a precise expression of confidence level
- **Morris's rule** (named after his friend Robert Morris): only publish views you're actually confident in; cut weak sentences; abandon branches that don't work

(Source: [How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html))

## 8. Literary influences and stylistic lineage

### 8.1 Influences PG has cited himself

| Influence | PG's assessment | Dimension of influence |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **P.G. Wodehouse** | "Wodehouse is so good that I get distracted by his perfection. Not a word wrong." | his primary model for writing — precise vocabulary + perfect rhythm |
| **20th-century British writers** | he likes how "directly they wrote" | the plain-style tradition |
| **Francis Bacon** | Henry Oliver draws the comparison between PG and Bacon | the exploratory essay, grounded in direct experience, ranging widely across topics |
| **Mathematicians/scientists/engineers** | PG says himself "the people who influenced how I think weren't writers, they were these people" | influence on his way of thinking (not his manner of expression) |

### 8.2 His place in the stylistic lineage

Henry Oliver places PG within the following tradition of English prose:
- An heir of the **plain style**
- Grouped alongside Evelyn Waugh and Nancy Mitford — using language with the air of "owning" English
- Essentially a fusion of **logically cut structure + chatty language**

(Source: [Henry Oliver's analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

### 8.3 The quotes PG collects (a clue to his style)

PG maintains a [quotes page](https://www.paulgraham.com/quo.html) on his own site, featuring lines from Churchill, Feynman, Munger, Alan Kay, and others. A notable commonality: every one of these quotes is extremely concise, forceful, and unconventional. This reflects PG's own aesthetic standard.

## 9. Map of core themes

The themes PG returns to repeatedly form his "intellectual territory":

| Theme cluster | Representative essays | Core argument |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Independent thinking vs. conformity** | What You Can't Say, Keep Your Identity Small | conventional wisdom is often wrong; independent thought is a scarce resource |
| **Startup methodology** | Do Things That Don't Scale, How to Get Startup Ideas | do the counterintuitive thing, start from small problems |
| **Writing as thinking** | Putting Ideas into Words, Writes and Write-Nots | writing isn't just expressing existing ideas — it's a tool for generating new ones |
| **Wealth and inequality** | Economic Inequality, How to Make Wealth | earning wealth by creating value is legitimate |
| **Critique of education** | Why Nerds Are Unpopular, What You'll Wish You'd Known | school is an artificial environment, not the real world |
| **Taste and design** | Taste for Makers, Hackers and Painters | good taste can be cultivated; programming is a craft |
| **AI and the future of writing** | Writes and Write-Nots (2024) | AI will divide the world into "those who write" and "those who don't" — that is, "those who think" and "those who don't" |

## 10. Never-do list: what PG absolutely never does

Based on a comprehensive analysis of 200+ essays and his Twitter activity:

1. **Never uses academic jargon**: no methodology, utilize, facilitate, paradigm
2. **Never stacks adjectives**: at most one adjective per noun
3. **Never hides the subject behind passive voice**: always makes clear "who did what"
4. **Never writes a "summary" paragraph**: an essay's ending is usually the last point landing naturally, not a recap of the whole piece
5. **Never cites a famous quote as evidence**: argues with his own logic and examples
6. **Never uses emoji/hashtags** (true on Twitter too)
7. **Never writes empty praise**: his compliments are always specific ("not a word wrong" rather than "great writer")
8. **Never uses decorative transition words**: no "furthermore," "moreover," "in addition"
9. **Never writes a listicle-style piece**: even when making several points, presents them as continuous prose
10. **Dislikes AI-contaminated words** like "delve" and "burgeoning"

## 11. Runnable style parameters

Quantified parameters for use in the skill template:

```yaml
sentence_length:
  average: 15-20 words (estimated, leans short but not extremely short)
  range: 5-40 words, alternating short and long to build rhythm
  signature: a short sentence stating the claim + a long sentence unfolding the example

paragraph_length:
  average: 3-5 sentences
  max: rarely exceeds 8 sentences

vocabulary:
  preference: simple Germanic/Anglo-Saxon words
  latinate_ratio: comparable to peers (roughly 25-30%), but subjectively reads as simpler
  forbidden: delve, burgeoning, utilize, methodology, paradigm, synergy
  signature_words: think, seem, probably, roughly, most, tend, usually

rhetorical_density:
  analogy_per_1000_words: 2-3 analogies
  example_frequency: ~70% of essays contain "for example"
  question_frequency: moderate, used for self-posed-question argumentation
  humor_per_essay: 2-4 instances of dry humor/irony

first_person:
  frequency: high — heavy use of "I"
  style: personal experience as a starting point, in service of a general claim
  hedging: frequently uses "I think," "I suspect," "it seems"

certainty_spectrum:
  strong_claims: "bold, but true" — very direct when confident
  qualified_claims: frequent use of probably/usually/most as hedges
  never: never uses absolute words like "absolutely," "definitely," "without doubt"

opening_style:
  preferred: personal anecdote > common wisdom + turn > direct claim > self-posed question
  never: celebrity quotes, definitions, rhetorical questions as a device

closing_style:
  preferred: the last point lands naturally
  never: a summary recap, a call to action, an exclamatory ending

twitter_style:
  length: 1-3 sentences
  structure: aphoristic / analogy-based / contrastive
  tone: sharper and more provocative than his essays
  emoji: never used
  hashtag: never used
```

## 12. Extractable classic sentence-template skeletons

Reusable sentence skeletons extracted from PG's work:

1. **Counterintuitive reveal**: "The way to [X] is not to [obvious approach]. It's to [counterintuitive approach]."
2. **A common blind spot**: "Most people [common belief]. But [contrarian truth]."
3. **An unexpected finding**: "It turns out that [surprising fact]."
4. **Argument by analogy**: "[Abstract concept] is like [concrete analogy]. [Extend the analogy 1-2 sentences]."
5. **Conditional causation**: "If you [condition], you [consequence]. Because [reasoning]."
6. **Self-posed question and answer**: "Why [question]? Because [answer that leads to next question]."
7. **Hedged assertion**: "I think/suspect [bold claim]. [Evidence or reasoning]."
8. **Parallel statement**: "[Before X, Y]. [After X, Z]." or "[Not A, but B]."
9. **Extreme form**: "The [superlative] [noun] are [unexpected predicate]."
10. **A concise definition**: "[Concept] is [short, surprising definition]."
11. **Leading with an example**: "[Anecdote in 1-2 sentences]. [Generalize to principle]."
12. **A deepening turn**: "[Statement A]. But [deeper truth that complicates A]."
