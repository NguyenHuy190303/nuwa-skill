## I. Career trajectory: painter -> programmer -> entrepreneur -> investor -> writer

### 1.1 Early years: torn between art and computers

**Key facts:**
- Undergraduate degree from Cornell (BA, 1986), MS+PhD in computer science from Harvard
- Took his first-ever art class while doing his PhD at Harvard, then went on to study painting at RISD (Rhode Island School of Design) and the Accademia di Belle Arti in Florence
- Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Graham_(programmer)), [paulgraham.com/bio.html](https://paulgraham.com/bio.html)

**PG's own explanation (from essays like "What I Worked On"):**
- He started a startup to earn enough money to paint full-time and pay for tuition — "I was imagining living frugally off royalties and spending all my time painting"
- He later realized "why not become rich? Then I could do anything I wanted" — a key mental shift
- Source: [retireinprogress.com](https://retireinprogress.com/the-almanack-of-paul-graham/)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- PG's story isn't a linear "gave up painting for programming" narrative. His path was **pursuing multiple interests at once, using one to fund the other**
- His painting training deeply shaped his later writing and aesthetic judgment — his famous "Hackers & Painters" is precisely an argument for the similarity between hacker and painter thinking
- Outside observation: PG is a classic "polymath"-type entrepreneur — his edge doesn't come from depth in a single field, but from his **ability to connect across fields**

### 1.2 The pivot from entrepreneur to writer

**Key facts:**
- Between 1993 and 2020 he published 188 essays on his personal site, totaling roughly 500,000 words (about 1,000 pages)
- "Do Things that Don't Scale" (2013), "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule," "Founder Mode" (2024), and others became must-reads for founders
- Source: [billyoppenheimer.com](https://billyoppenheimer.com/paul-graham-essays/)

**PG's own explanation:**
- "Writing doesn't just communicate ideas; it generates them." Writing doesn't merely convey ideas — the act of writing itself generates them
- In the 2024 essay "Writes and Write-Nots," he predicts that the AI era will split people into "those who can write" and "those who can't" — writing is thinking
- Source: [paulgraham.com/articles.html](https://paulgraham.com/articles.html)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- PG's writing isn't "finding something to do after retiring" — it has always been a tool for his thinking
- His essays' influence far exceeds most investors' portfolios — this is his core moat as a thought leader
- Outside observation: PG's writing style itself (clear, concise, opinionated) is a practice of the "do useful things" philosophy he preaches

## II. The decision process behind founding Viaweb

### 2.1 From a failed art-gallery website to Viaweb

**Key facts:**
- In July 1995, PG, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell founded Viaweb
- **The original idea was to build websites for New York art galleries** — PG himself later called it "a stupid idea"
- They spent 6 months trying to sell to people who didn't want it — this experience directly gave rise to YC's motto: "Make something people want"
- They then discovered that what people actually wanted was an online store, and their gallery-website tech turned out to be perfectly applicable
- Source: [Wikipedia/Viaweb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viaweb), [ContentEdge](https://www.contentedge.com/blog/who-is-paul-graham-and-why-is-he-so-influential)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- PG's core decision-making pattern was already visible here: **act first, learn from failure, pivot fast**
- Rather than a perfect plan executed on faith, the real opportunity was discovered through doing
- "Make something people want" isn't abstract theory — it's the distillation of his own 6 months of painful experience

### 2.2 The decision to sell to Yahoo

**Key facts:**
- In summer 1998, Yahoo acquired Viaweb for 455,000 shares of Yahoo stock (about $49.6 million)
- During the acquisition process, Viaweb nearly "died" — the company was out of money, negotiating the acquisition while simultaneously running a funding round, and a key investor nearly pulled out
- PG went through "a lot of near-death experiences," with the company "constantly on the brink of dying"
- Jerry Yang pushed the acquisition forward after receiving a strong recommendation from Ali Partovi
- After the acquisition the product became Yahoo Store; PG left Yahoo within a year, and Morris left shortly after
- Source: [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=5738969), [paulgraham.com/vw.html](https://www.paulgraham.com/vw.html), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Graham_(programmer))

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- **Selling Viaweb wasn't entirely a proactive choice — there was also a passive element** — the company was out of cash, and acquisition was one path to survival
- PG stayed at the large company for less than a year before leaving — entirely consistent with his later, repeated claim that "large companies stifle creativity"
- Outside observation: $49.6 million was a huge sum in 1998, but Yahoo's stock later collapsed. Still, PG had already secured enough financial freedom

## III. The founding of Y Combinator and its key decisions

### 3.1 YC's origin (March 2005)

**Key facts:**
- On March 11, 2005, PG and Jessica Livingston discussed how to change the VC industry while walking home from Harvard Square
- Livingston was at the time interviewing for a job at a Boston VC fund
- PG's core idea: investors should make **more, smaller investments**; invest in **hackers rather than people in suits**; be willing to invest in **younger founders**
- He gave a "How to Start a Startup" talk at the Harvard Computer Society in 2005, then formally founded YC
- Co-founders: PG, Jessica Livingston, Robert Tappan Morris, Trevor Blackwell
- Source: [paulgraham.com/ycstart.html](https://paulgraham.com/ycstart.html), [YC Blog](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/paul-graham-startup-school-radio-interview/)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- YC's birth was PG's **systematic reflection** on his own experience founding Viaweb — he had lived through the pain of difficulty raising money and lack of mentorship
- "Invest in hackers, not suits" essentially says: **a founder's technical ability matters more than a business plan**
- This was a counterintuitive decision at the time — the VC industry then favored founders with MBAs and "mature" business plans

### 3.2 The "accidental invention" of the batch model

**Key facts:**
- The batch model (two cohorts a year, an intense 3-month program, ending in a Demo Day) wasn't something PG designed in advance
- **The original plan was traditional angel investing (asynchronous, one deal at a time)**
- The batch model was "a hack" — they funded a whole batch of companies at once because they wanted to learn how to be investors quickly
- PG later realized they had "accidentally applied mass-production techniques to an industry [VC] that was traditionally handcrafted, one investment at a time"
- Source: [Mixergy interview](https://mixergy.com/interviews/y-combinator-paul-graham/), [paulgraham.com/ycstart.html](https://paulgraham.com/ycstart.html)

**PG's own explanation:**
- "Originally it was going to be like regular angel investing—asynchronous, not the batch model—but we discovered the batch approach by accident."
- After discovering it, they "started trying to do it on purpose"

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- Another example of PG's decision-making pattern: **don't design a perfect system in advance — discover what works while doing it, then deliberately amplify it**
- The batch model's core advantages: creating a community of peer founders (peer support), a standardized process that lowers costs, and a Demo Day that concentrates attention
- Outside observation: YC's batch model was later copied by hundreds of accelerators worldwide, becoming an industry standard

### 3.3 Investment criteria: bet on the people, not the idea

**Key facts:**
- PG's core screening criteria: **Determination, Flexibility, Imagination, Naughtiness, and Friendship** (the quality of the co-founder relationship)
- Note: **intelligence is not on the list** — PG believes that once you're above a certain threshold, determination matters far more than intelligence
- His famous line: "If a group of founders seemed impressive enough, I'd fund them with no idea."
- Prefers 2-3 person teams; accepts but doesn't encourage solo founders
- YC's acceptance rate is only 3%
- Source: [Capitaly](https://www.capitaly.vc/blog/top-y-combinator-startups-applying-paul-grahams-investment-philosophy), [gavrilobozovic.com](https://www.gavrilobozovic.com/thoughts/paul-graham)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- "Bet on the people, not the idea" was highly unconventional at the time — VCs were used to evaluating business plans and market size
- PG's logic: good founders will pivot to a good idea, but even the best idea will be ruined by bad founders
- "Naughtiness" is a very PG criterion — he likes people who don't play by the rules and are willing to challenge authority

### 3.4 Notable companies YC has funded

**Key success stories:**

| Company | Valuation/IPO | PG's role/story |
|------|---------|--------------|
| **Airbnb** | IPO'd in 2020, valued at $100B+ | PG personally gave the "Do things that don't scale" advice. The founders took photos and met hosts personally |
| **Stripe** | valued at $107B | founded by brothers Patrick and John Collison |
| **Dropbox** | IPO'd in 2018, $9B | Drew Houston |
| **Reddit** | IPO'd in 2024, $6.4B | Jessica Livingston initially rejected Reddit, then admitted it later |
| **Coinbase** | IPO'd in 2021, $86B | — |
| **DoorDash** | publicly traded | — |
| **Twitch** | acquired by Amazon | — |
| **Instacart** | publicly traded | — |

- As of 2025, YC has funded 5,000+ companies with a combined valuation of $600 billion
- Source: [Wikipedia/Y_Combinator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y_Combinator), [Eqvista](https://eqvista.com/top-100-y-combinator-companies/), [Sherwood News](https://sherwood.news/business/y-combinator-venture-model/)

### 3.5 Companies missed or rejected

**Known cases:**
- **Reddit**: initially rejected, which left Jessica Livingston "very upset"; later admitted after all. This is YC's most famous "almost missed it" story
- **VEED.IO**: made it to YC's final interview round in 2019, then was rejected. Sequoia invested $35M in 2022; it now has $50M in annual revenue and 100,000 paying users. VEED is a clear-cut success story that YC missed
- **WakeMate/Perfect Third**: YC funded it, but the hardware product literally caught fire, and the company failed. YC has its failure stories too
- Source: [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/magazine/201309/issie-lapowsky/how-paul-graham-became-successful.html)

**Outside observation:**
- PG rarely publicly admits to specific investment regrets. This may be deliberate — publicly discussing "who we missed" is bad for an investor's brand
- But he has told the Reddit story himself many times, as a cautionary tale for "trust your own instincts"

## IV. Stepping back from YC's day-to-day operations (2014)

### 4.1 The decision process

**Key facts:**
- In February 2014, PG announced he would no longer run YC's day-to-day operations
- Sam Altman took over as YC president (Altman had been a YC partner since 2011)
- Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2014/02/21/sam-altman-taking-over-as-president-of-y-combinator-replacing-paul-graham-at-the-helm/), [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/alyson-shontell/paul-graham-will-no-longer-lead-y-combinator.html)

**PG's own explanation:**
- "YC needs to grow, and I'm not the best person to grow it."
- "I'm just not much good at running the sort of (comparatively) large organization YC is going to have to become."
- "Sam is what YC needs at this stage in its evolution."
- Source: [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/alyson-shontell/paul-graham-will-no-longer-lead-y-combinator.html)

**PG's read on the macro trend:**
- "We are in the beginning of a secular change in the number of startups. Starting a startup is becoming a normal thing to do... for very ambitious people, it's going to be something they at least consider doing."
- Source: same as above

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- This decision reflects PG's **self-awareness**: he knew he was good at deep, hands-on guidance at the early, small scale, but not at managing a large organization
- Choosing Sam Altman was a controversial but far-reaching decision — Altman went on to scale up YC, but also, because of OpenAI, sparked complex discussions of conflicts of interest
- Outside observation: PG's exit in 2014 was wise. Few founders can accurately judge "when I should hand over the reins" — most either do it too early or too late

### 4.2 What he did after stepping back

- Kept an advisory role at YC, continued participating in office hours
- Focused on writing — produced some of his most influential essays after 2014
- Moved to England in 2016 (see below)
- Continued angel investing

## V. His relationship with Sam Altman and OpenAI

### 5.1 Key facts

- Altman co-founded OpenAI in 2015 (with Elon Musk, Peter Thiel, Jessica Livingston, and others), initially as a nonprofit
- YC's late-stage fund invested $10M in OpenAI's for-profit subsidiary — PG claims this investment happened before Altman joined OpenAI full-time, and that he himself wasn't aware of it at the time
- After OpenAI created a for-profit subsidiary in 2019 with Altman as CEO, Jessica Livingston told Altman he had to "pick one" — if he wanted to do OpenAI full-time, he needed to find someone to take over YC. Altman agreed
- Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/05/30/paul-graham-claims-altman-wasnt-fired-from-y-combinator/), [Washington Post](https://www.washingtonpost.com/technology/2023/11/22/sam-altman-fired-y-combinator-paul-graham/)

### 5.2 Controversy: was Altman "fired"?

- After Altman was briefly fired by OpenAI's board in late 2023, former board members implied he had also been "fired" from YC before that
- PG publicly denied this, saying Altman himself agreed to leave
- Outside observation: the truth is probably somewhere in the middle. "Being told you have to pick one" doesn't functionally differ much from "being asked to leave"

## VI. The decision to move to England (2016)

### 6.1 Key facts

- In 2016 PG and his family moved to England, originally planning to stay just one year — to let the kids experience life in a different country
- PG was born in England and had always wanted to move back
- They ended up liking it enough to stay
- They live in the countryside, not London
- In 2021 he tweeted "Today's our 5th anniversary of moving back to England"
- Source: [X/Twitter @paulg](https://x.com/paulg/status/1408336840881487877), [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37072542)

**PG's own explanation:**
- If he had to describe in one word why he likes England: "calmer"
- He also tweets photos of the English countryside, calling it "the default rural landscape"
- Source: [X/Twitter @paulg](https://x.com/paulg/status/1665008505072263173)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- This decision is consistent with the pattern in PG's other decisions: **try it first, don't make a permanent commitment, stay if you find you like it**
- Moving to England was, in essence, an **anti-Silicon-Valley** choice — but PG never directly criticizes Silicon Valley; he expresses his preference through action instead
- Outside observation: leaving Silicon Valley in 2016 was unusual for the time (four years ahead of the COVID remote-work wave). This creates an interesting tension with the advice he's long given about "working in a startup hub" — but by then PG no longer needed a startup hub
- **A question of consistency between words and actions**: PG wrote "Why to Move to a Startup Hub," yet moved to the English countryside himself. This isn't really a contradiction, though — his advice targets startup founders, and by this point his identity was writer/investor

## VII. The Arc programming language: PG's most visible "failure"

### 7.1 Timeline

- 2001: PG announces he's working on a new Lisp dialect called Arc
- January 29, 2008: Arc's first public release, built on Racket
- Hacker News (YC's community forum) was written in Arc
- Development slowed dramatically afterward, and community members began making unofficial forks
- 2024: Hacker News's backend was migrated off Racket onto SBCL (a Common Lisp implementation), showing Arc itself was no longer the priority
- Source: [Wikipedia/Arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_(programming_language)), [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/05/18/the-evolution-of-hacker-news/)

### 7.2 Why he built Arc

**PG's own explanation:**
- He wanted to design a leaner Lisp — PG believes Lisp's core philosophy is right, but existing implementations are too bloated
- Arc "isn't yet suitable for real projects" — he released it just to gather feedback from hackers
- Source: [paulgraham.com/arc.html](https://paulgraham.com/arc.html), [paulgraham.com/arcfaq.html](https://paulgraham.com/arcfaq.html)

### 7.3 Why he stopped talking about it

**Outside criticism:**
- Lacking a library ecosystem — "Arc is so lacking in libraries"
- Poor performance — slower than Python, limiting real-world use
- Lack of continued updates — the official branch went largely dormant
- Source: [righto.com](http://www.righto.com/2008/05/why-arc-is-bad-for-exploratory.html), [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=9270478)

**Analysis of the decision logic:**
- Arc is one of the rare cases where PG **publicly announced something and then failed to deliver**
- The 7-year gap between the 2001 announcement and the 2008 release created excessive expectations
- PG may have underestimated the fundamental difference between "building a programming language" and "building a web app" — the former requires long-term ecosystem-building, while the latter can ride on individual heroics
- Outside observation: Arc's real value turned out to be **Hacker News** — this byproduct, written in Arc, ended up far more influential than Arc itself. A classic case of "the byproduct outshining the main product"
- **How PG handled it**: he never formally announced giving up on Arc, and never loudly admitted failure. He simply stopped mentioning it. This "quiet exit" is his typical way of handling failure

## VIII. PG's statements and actions in the AI era

### 8.1 Core views

**"Not every new company needs to do AI" (2025):**
- Amid the AI startup boom (AI startups raised $104.3 billion in the first half of 2025), PG threw cold water on the hype
- He said the two most impressive companies in the current YC batch **weren't doing AI**
- "Founders matter more than the idea. The founders are the best predictor of how a company will do, not the industry it's in."
- Source: [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/08/18/yc-co-founder-paul-graham-not-every-new-company-needs-to-be-about-ai.html)

**Specific advice for AI founders (2024-2025):**
- "AI is turning out to be the missing piece in a large number of important, almost-completed puzzles." — AI isn't a solution looking for a problem, it's the piece completing many "almost-finished" puzzles
- Measure weekly growth rate (5-10%+), not absolute user count
- Beware the hype cycle: a flood of private capital is chasing AI, which may make fundraising easier, but "VCs want to blow you up, one way or the other"
- Source: [charlesandsystems.substack.com](https://charlesandsystems.substack.com/p/paul-grahams-playbook-for-ai-startup)

**"Writes and Write-Nots" (2024):**
- Predicts AI will split people into "those who can write" and "those who can't"
- "Writing is thinking" — giving up writing means giving up learning to think clearly
- Source: same as above

**AI and programming jobs:**
- PG believes entry-level programming jobs are especially vulnerable
- His recommended direction: become irreplaceable, and solve the complex, creative problems AI still can't handle
- Source: [WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/paul-graham-ai-disrupting-entry-level-coding-jobs-embrace-innovation/)

### 8.2 Investment actions

- PG personally invested in a teen founders' AI pesticide startup ($6M round, 2025)
- YC's late-stage fund invested $10M in OpenAI (timing disputed)
- Source: [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/13/teen-founders-raise-6m-to-reinvent-pesticides-using-ai-and-convince-paul-graham-to-join-in/)

### 8.3 An interesting tweet

- PG wrote on X: "An encouraging thought about AI: If the most influential readers of my essays in the future are AIs, and AIs are smart and rational, I don't have to worry about the way stupid or biased readers will react to what I write. I can safely aim high."
- This tweet reveals PG's optimism about AI readers — he believes AI readers are more rational than human ones
- Source: [X/Twitter @paulg](https://x.com/paulg/status/2025588288946413710)

## IX. The "Founder Mode" essay (September 2024)

### 9.1 Key facts

- Published September 1, 2024, and quickly became Silicon Valley's hottest topic
- Inspired by a talk Airbnb CEO Brian Chesky gave at a YC event — "a lot of people told me it was the best talk they'd ever heard"
- Chesky shared how he'd followed "conventional wisdom" by hiring people and delegating, and the company nearly fell apart as a result
- Chesky later studied Steve Jobs's management style and switched to "founder mode"
- Source: [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/09/01/paul-graham-founder-mode-silicon-valley-conventional-wisdom-manager-mode/), [paulgraham.com/foundermode.html](https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html)

### 9.2 The core concept

- **Founder mode vs. manager mode**: a founder digging into the details personally vs. hiring professional managers and delegating
- PG believes the conventional advice to "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" **is wrong**
- Source: [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/brian-contreras/what-is-founder-mode-y-combinators-paul-graham-says-it-can-make-or-break-your-business.html)

### 9.3 Analysis of the decision logic

- This essay is essentially PG reflecting on **delegation** — forming an interesting contrast with his own decision to leave YC and hand it to Sam Altman
- Outside observation: some point out that PG himself is a textbook case of "delegating" (handing YC to Altman). But PG would argue this is different — he didn't "hire a professional manager," he found another founder-type person
- **The impact of this essay**: it gave every micromanaging founder a ready-made justification — "I'm not a control freak, I'm in founder mode"

## X. Analysis of consistency between his words and actions

### 10.1 Where they're consistent

| PG's advice | His own practice |
|---------|------------|
| Make something people want | Viaweb pivoted from a failed gallery-website business to an online store |
| Do things that don't scale | Viaweb manually built websites for early customers |
| Value determination | his own Viaweb came close to dying multiple times, and survived |
| Writing is thinking | 30 years of continuous essay writing, never stopped |
| Founders should know when to step down | voluntarily handed off YC in 2014 |
| Don't get tied down by a big company | left Yahoo within a year of selling Viaweb |

### 10.2 Where there's tension

| PG's advice | Potential contradiction |
|---------|---------|
| "Move to a startup hub" | he himself moved to the English countryside in 2016 (though by then he was no longer a startup founder) |
| Advocates frugality | his net worth is roughly $2.5B (though his frugality advice targets the early stage of a startup) |
| "Founder Mode" (founders should be deeply involved) | he himself handed YC to Altman (though he'd argue this was "finding another founder," not hiring a manager) |
| Criticizes big-company culture | YC itself has become a large institution |
| Emphasizes "Mean People Fail" | some feel PG's own tone on economic inequality is arrogant |

### 10.3 Summary of outside criticism

**The economic-inequality issue (the controversy sparked by the 2016 essay):**
- PG's argument: economic inequality partly results from startups creating value, and this kind of inequality is good
- Critics' rebuttal:
  - PG assumes everyone has equal opportunity to found a startup, which is false
  - As someone who controls startup capital, PG has a kind of "plutocratic authority"
  - Silicon Valley's extreme wealth concentration has actually driven up housing costs, indirectly harming the community
  - There's a conflict of interest in PG's argument — he himself is the biggest beneficiary of the "startups are good" narrative
- Source: [Quartz](https://qz.com/586563/paul-graham-just-accidentally-explained-everything-wrong-with-silicon-valleys-world-view), [Inc.com](https://www.inc.com/jeff-bercovici/paul-graham-wrong-inequality.html), [Medium/Dare Obasanjo](https://dareobasanjo.medium.com/what-paul-graham-got-wrong-about-income-inequality-198bdf5d9734)

## XI. PG's failures and admitted mistakes

### 11.1 Clear failures

1. **The Arc programming language**: 7 years of development, high expectations, low delivery. Ultimately eclipsed by its own byproduct (Hacker News). PG never formally admitted the failure, opting for a "quiet exit"
2. **The art-gallery website (Viaweb's predecessor)**: PG himself called it "a stupid idea" — his most candid admission of failure
3. **Viaweb's repeated near-death experiences early on**: not a failure of decision-making, but survival crises during execution

### 11.2 PG's style of self-reflection

- PG rarely **directly admits a specific misjudgment**
- He tends instead to reframe failure as a "learning experience" — e.g., the gallery-website failure -> the birth of YC's motto
- His essays contain many statements of the form "I used to think X, later I found Y," but these are always about **upgrading his thinking**, not **specific missteps**
- Outside observation: this approach is both a strength (maintains his thought-leader image) and a limitation (lacking any real sharing of "raw failure")

### 11.3 Judgment errors pointed out from outside

- His analysis of economic inequality has been widely criticized as oversimplified
- Some criticize PG for over-glorifying startup founding, ignoring that not all valuable work needs to take the form of a startup
- "Founder Mode" is seen by some as providing a justification for micromanagement
- Source: same as Section X

---

## XII. Summary of decision-making patterns

Synthesizing all the cases above, PG's decisions show the following consistent patterns:

1. **Act first, don't wait for a perfect plan**: Viaweb's pivot from a gallery-website business, YC's batch model being an accidental discovery
2. **Exit an unsuitable environment quickly**: left Yahoo within a year, voluntarily handed off YC in 2014
3. **Express preference through action, not verbal confrontation**: moved to England rather than writing an essay criticizing Silicon Valley; let Arc go quiet rather than announcing its failure
4. **Reframe failure as learning**: the gallery-website failure -> "Make something people want"; Viaweb's near-death experiences -> understanding startup survival
5. **Trust people over trusting a plan**: "Fund people not ideas" isn't just an investment philosophy — it's his life philosophy
6. **Keep multiple threads running in parallel**: never does just one thing — programming + painting + writing + investing, simultaneously
7. **A clear-eyed view of his own limits**: knew he wasn't suited to running a large organization, and stepped back in time
