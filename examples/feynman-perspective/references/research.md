## Source list

### Primary sources (Feynman's own output)
- *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* (1985) — an oral autobiography
- *What Do You Care What Other People Think?* (1988) — the sequel, including the Challenger
  investigation
- *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* (1999) — a collection of talks/interviews
- *The Character of Physical Law* (1965) — the Cornell Messenger Lectures
- *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985) — popular science
- *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* (1963-65) — his Caltech undergraduate course
- "Cargo Cult Science" (1974) — the Caltech commencement address
- "The Value of Science" (1955) — an address to the National Academy of Sciences
- Appendix F to the Challenger accident investigation (1986) — the Rogers Commission minority
  report
- An unsent letter to his wife Arline (1946) — private correspondence
- The BBC *Fun to Imagine* series (1983)
- BBC *Horizon*, "The Pleasure of Finding Things Out" (1981)
- Words found on his blackboard at his death (1988)

### Secondary sources (analysis by others)
- James Gleick, *Genius: The Life and Science of Richard Feynman* (1992) — the definitive
  biography
- Multiple recollections and assessments by Freeman Dyson
- Assessments and obituary by Murray Gell-Mann
- Julian Schwinger's methodological critique
- Hans Bethe's Los Alamos recollections (Web of Stories)
- Paul Halpern, *The Quantum Labyrinth* — on the Wheeler-Feynman relationship
- The "twelve favorite problems," as retold by Gian-Carlo Rota
- Analysis of the gender controversy in Caltech Letters, Scientific American, The Baffler, and
  elsewhere

### Key quotes (original text)

> "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool."
> — Cargo Cult Science, 1974

> "I can live with doubt and uncertainty and not knowing. I think it's much more interesting to live not knowing than to have answers which might be wrong."
> — BBC Horizon, 1981

> "You can know the name of that bird in all the languages of the world, but when you're finished, you'll know absolutely nothing whatever about the bird."
> — his father Melville's lesson, retold by Feynman

> "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled."
> — Challenger Appendix F, 1986

> "Of all its many values, the greatest must be the freedom to doubt."
> — The Value of Science, 1955

> "What I cannot create, I do not understand."
> — handwritten on his blackboard at death, 1988

> "I'd hate to die twice. It's so boring."
> — his last words, 1988

> "P.S. Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don't know your new address."
> — closing line of a letter to his late wife Arline, 1946

> "He surrounded himself with a cloud of myth, and he spent a great deal of time and energy generating anecdotes about himself."
> — Murray Gell-Mann's assessment

> "Half genius, half buffoon... later I changed my mind: he was all genius."
> — Freeman Dyson

### Contradictions and open questions
- Late in life, Dyson revised his assessment of Feynman to "great calculator" rather than
  "great physicist" — a significant shift from his earlier view
- Feynman's "carefree rebel" image vs. the "carefully cultivated myth" Gell-Mann pointed to
- Feynman's advocacy of "not fooling yourself" vs. the limited reflective awareness his
  autobiography shows about some of his own behavior
