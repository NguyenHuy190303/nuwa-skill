# Richard Feynman (1918-1988) — Major-Decisions Research

> Research date: 2026-04-04
> Research target: the major decisions, turning points, and controversial actions across
> Feynman's life
> Note: [fact] marks verified information; [inference] marks a reasonable inference from the
> facts

---

## 1. Joining the Manhattan Project (1943, age 25)

**Event**: Bob Wilson found Feynman at Princeton and told him about a secret project —
separating uranium isotopes to build an atomic bomb.

**Decision**: hesitated at first, ultimately decided to join.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] his core motivation was fear that Germany would build the bomb first. Feynman later
  said explicitly that what drove him was "the fear that Nazi Germany would get nuclear
  weapons first"
- [Fact] Bob Wilson's persuasion tactic was clever — he said, "I know once you understand what
  this project is doing, you'll see you have to be part of it"
- [Inference] Feynman was early in his academic career at the time (just starting his PhD at
  Princeton); this project brought together America's top physicists, which for a young man
  was both a calling and an opportunity

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later expressed regret: after Germany surrendered, he never reexamined his
  reason for continuing on the project. "The reason that got me in there had stopped
  existing, but I didn't think about it at all, I just kept going"
- [Fact] the brief euphoria after the successful Trinity test turned quickly to deep unease
- [Inference] this experience shaped Feynman's later fixation on "never stop thinking" — he'd
  personally experienced the danger of inertial thinking

## 2. The death of his wife, Arline (June 1945)

**Event**: Feynman's childhood sweetheart and first wife, Arline Greenbaum, died of
tuberculosis at age 25 — a month before the Trinity test.

**Decision**: Feynman processed the grief in an intensely rational way — he went straight back
to work, and didn't cry for the first time until months later.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman's own account: he did something "psychological" to himself — because reality
  mattered too much to him, he needed to first understand, on a physiological level, what was
  happening in Arline's body, and this process of rationalization blocked the emotional
  reaction
- [Fact] in October 1946 (16 months after her death), Feynman wrote a letter to Arline; it was
  sealed and not opened until after Feynman's own death in 1988
- [Fact] the letter reads: "I find it hard to understand what it means to love you after you
  are dead — but I still want to comfort you and take care of you — and I want you to love me
  and care for me"
- [Fact] the letter's closing line: "P.S. Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don't know
  your new address."

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Inference] Arline's death, compounded by the moral shock of the Manhattan Project, drove
  Feynman into a severe psychological low after the war (he later said he could barely work
  during his first years at Cornell)
- [Fact] Feynman later said Arline "stood in the way of his falling in love with anyone else"
  — this emotional imprint lasted a long time
- [Inference] this unsent letter may be one of the most moving love letters of the 20th century,
  showing the "intensely emotional interior beneath the rational shell" side of Feynman

## 3. Leaving Cornell, choosing Caltech (around 1950)

**Event**: after the Manhattan Project, Feynman taught at Cornell, but considered leaving.

**Decision**: accepted Caltech's offer, and never left for the rest of his life.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] in *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*, he says the direct trigger for leaving was
  putting snow chains on his car during an Ithaca blizzard, when the thought "I want to live
  somewhere with warm weather" lodged firmly in his mind
- [Fact] a year as a visiting scholar in Brazil reinforced his preference for warm climates
- [Fact] Caltech's academic atmosphere suited him — Feynman said that just walking across
  campus, colleagues would run up to tell him about the latest breakthrough
- [Inference] on the surface Feynman moved "because of the weather," but a deeper reason may
  be that his time at Cornell overlapped with his psychological low point (Arline's death,
  postwar confusion), and a completely new environment carried the sense of a psychological
  reset

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later became intensely anxious whenever another university made him an offer,
  and eventually made a decision: **never again consider working anywhere else**, no matter
  how tempting the salary or terms
- [Inference] this "close it once and for all" decision pattern is very Feynman — rather than
  repeatedly spending energy re-deciding, close the option off once. This is highly consistent
  with Buffett's principle of "don't let someone spend your time for you"

## 4. Developing Feynman diagrams and the path integral (1948)

**Event**: in quantum electrodynamics (QED), Feynman developed two revolutionary tools: the
path-integral method and Feynman diagrams.

**Decision**: chose a methodological path entirely different from the mainstream. The
mainstream at the time was operator algebra (canonical quantization); Feynman took an
intuitive, visual path instead.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] the core idea of the path integral: a particle traveling from A to B doesn't take one
  path — it takes "every possible path," and the final result is the superposition of all of
  them. The idea came from a hint in Dirac's work, which Feynman developed into a complete
  mathematical framework
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams turned what had been extremely tedious operator-algebra calculations
  into "draw a diagram on a napkin, apply the rules" — and you get precise numerical
  predictions
- [Inference] Feynman's mind was naturally visual and intuition-driven — he disliked abstract
  formalism, preferring a method where "I can see what's happening." This is of a piece with
  his later philosophy of "if you can't explain it simply, you don't really understand it"

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams remain a basic tool for particle physicists today
- [Fact] the path-integral method later proved more elegant than traditional methods in many
  fields, including quantum field theory, statistical mechanics, and condensed-matter physics
- [Fact] in 1965, Feynman shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Schwinger and Tomonaga — three
  people who reached the same result through completely different methods
- [Inference] the success of Feynman diagrams shows that sometimes the most powerful
  innovation isn't discovering a new fact — it's inventing a new "way of seeing the problem"

## 5. Almost declining the Nobel Prize (1965)

**Event**: Feynman won the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on QED.

**Decision**: seriously considered declining it, ultimately accepted.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman's attitude toward the Nobel Prize was always ambivalent. He felt "the Nobel
  committee should ask your permission before giving you the award"
- [Fact] one reason he didn't want to accept it involved Lewis Strauss — Strauss had stripped
  Oppenheimer of his security clearance, and Feynman, angry about that, didn't want to accept
  an honor tied to that power structure
- [Fact] Isidor Rabi talked him down: "You should never turn a man's generosity as a sword
  against him"
- [Fact] the actual reason he finally accepted: he realized **declining the Nobel Prize would
  bring more attention and more trouble than accepting it**

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later said in his memoirs that he was "almost afraid" of winning, because he
  worried he'd never again produce work worth noticing
- [Inference] this fear reflects Feynman's wariness of "identity lock-in" — he didn't want to
  be defined by a label. This is consistent with his pattern of declining honorary degrees and
  various titles
- [Fact] Feynman turned down honorary degrees, saying "I don't want people spending my time on
  things that don't mean anything"

## 6. His teaching experience in Brazil and his criticism of education (1952, 1963)

**Event**: Feynman spent a year in Brazil (around 1952), and in 1963 gave an address titled
"The Problem of Teaching Physics in Latin America" at the first Inter-American Conference on
Physics Education in Rio.

**Decision**: publicly declared at the conference that "Brazil isn't teaching science at all,"
directly criticizing the entire education system.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman found students could answer a textbook question instantly, but couldn't
  answer at all if the same question was rephrased — they'd memorized words and formulas, not
  understanding
- [Fact] he criticized this as "a self-propagating system: people pass exams, then teach
  others to pass exams, but no one actually understands anything"
- [Fact] he found the "experimental data" in the textbooks was fabricated — the numbers looked
  like real experimental results (with small deviations), but were actually made up
- [Inference] Feynman's choice to criticize publicly rather than privately reflects his core
  belief: an obligation to the truth outweighs social politeness

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] this episode later became widely known through *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!*,
  becoming a classic case study in education criticism
- [Inference] Feynman's distaste for "rote memorization disguised as understanding" directly
  shaped how he later taught at Caltech — the famous *Feynman Lectures on Physics* was his
  attempt to reteach physics from the ground up

## 7. The Challenger accident investigation (1986)

**Event**: on January 28, 1986, the space shuttle Challenger exploded, killing all 7
astronauts. NASA formed the Rogers Commission to investigate; Feynman was a member.

**Decision**: Feynman chose to investigate independently, unconstrained by the commission's
bureaucratic process; at a televised hearing he ran a now-famous live demonstration with a
glass of ice water and a piece of O-ring rubber.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman found that Morton Thiokol's engineers had warned NASA management the day
  before launch that the O-ring seal would fail in cold conditions — but management ignored
  the engineers
- [Fact] NASA management had recorded the O-ring test results as "a safety factor of 3" — but
  Feynman found this description completely distorted the facts; they were using the word
  "safe" to describe a part that was actually flawed and unsafe
- [Fact] on live television, Feynman dropped the O-ring rubber into ice water to show it lost
  its elasticity at low temperature — this 10-second demonstration was more persuasive than
  several hundred pages of reports
- [Inference] Feynman's choice of "demonstration" over "argument" fits his epistemology
  exactly: if you can't make someone see it simply, you haven't really understood the problem
  yourself

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman's report was almost left out of the Rogers Commission's official report — he
  was asked to put it in an appendix. Feynman threatened to resign from the commission if it
  wasn't included
- [Fact] Feynman closed the report with the now-famous line: "For a successful technology,
  reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled."
- [Inference] the Challenger investigation shows several core features of Feynman's decision
  pattern: (1) thinking independently, not being led by institutional lines; (2) letting the
  experiment speak, not authority; (3) willingness to make enemies — he was, at bottom,
  publicly accusing NASA management of using wordplay to cover up a safety hazard

## 8. Competition and collaboration with Murray Gell-Mann

**Event**: Feynman and Gell-Mann were both at Caltech, two of the most outstanding theoretical
physicists of the latter half of the 20th century.

**Decision/behavior pattern**: both collaborated and competed, a complicated relationship.

**Key facts**:
- [Fact] in 1957-1958, the two collaborated on the V-A theory (vector minus axial vector) of
  weak interactions, while Sudarshan and Marshak independently discovered the same theory
- [Fact] both were from New York, but their styles were opposite: Feynman deliberately kept his
  working-class accent, while Gell-Mann pursued perfect pronunciation in every foreign word
- [Fact] Gell-Mann was famously a pedant and a walking encyclopedia of language and knowledge;
  Feynman was known for a fast, onomatopoeia-rich communication style, utterly unconcerned
  about who was listening
- [Fact] the two would trade "surprisingly childish remarks" about each other
- [Fact] after the summer of 1958, the two essentially stopped collaborating

**Inferred competitive logic**:
- [Inference] this was a collision between two entirely different "modes of genius":
  Gell-Mann was encyclopedic, systematic, chasing formal perfection; Feynman was
  intuition-driven, visual, chasing essential understanding
- [Inference] their rivalry may have made each of them better — having a top-tier rival close
  by pushed both to stay at the highest level

## 9. His attitude toward old age and cancer (1978-1988)

**Event**: Feynman battled cancer for 8 years starting in 1978, suffering successively from
liposarcoma and Waldenstrom's macroglobulinemia.

**Decision**: kept teaching until 15 days before his death; ultimately chose to stop treatment.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] his attitude toward his illness included refusing to prolong meaningless suffering,
  and facing everything he was going through without sentimentality
- [Fact] Feynman's death wasn't an accident — it was a complicated medical process, and a
  conscious choice about "when to stop fighting"
- [Fact] even as his body deteriorated, Feynman kept observing reality with curiosity —
  including the decline of his own body
- [Fact] his last words: "I'd hate to die twice. It's so boring."

**Outcome/later impact**:
- [Inference] this last line perfectly condenses Feynman's entire personality: humor in the
  face of death, distaste for boredom (implying the long wait of the dying process), and a
  kind of detachment that says "I've seen enough"
- [Inference] Feynman's attitude toward death was consistent with his attitude toward physics:
  don't avoid reality, don't gloss it over with pretty words, face things as they actually are

## 10. Cracking safes, playing the bongos, and other "not his real job"

**Event**: Feynman's list of "side pursuits" is remarkable — cracking safes at Los Alamos,
mastering the bongo drums, learning to draw, sketching at strip clubs, learning Mayan
hieroglyphics, and more.

**Decision/behavior pattern**: Feynman never considered these "not his real job" — he believed
curiosity shouldn't have a boundary.

**Key facts**:
- [Fact] at Los Alamos, out of pure curiosity, Feynman discovered a weakness in filing-cabinet
  locks, then left anonymous notes inside safes holding top-secret documents, terrifying
  everyone
- [Fact] Feynman was a professional-level bongo player, and it became part of his public image
- [Fact] when a Swedish publisher asked him for a photo of himself drumming to "make
  theoretical physics more human," Feynman pushed back directly: "my playing the drums has
  nothing whatsoever to do with my doing theoretical physics," and called the idea insulting
- [Inference] this response reveals a key stance of Feynman's: he wasn't "performing" an
  interesting scientist — playing drums was just playing drums, physics was just physics,
  neither needed to serve the other

**The deeper inferred logic**:
- [Inference] Feynman's "not his real job" pursuits actually reflect a cognitive strategy:
  staying active in completely different fields to prevent his thinking from hardening.
  Cracking safes trains pattern recognition and systematic trial and error — not fundamentally
  different from doing physics
- [Inference] Feynman's line "I was born not knowing and have had only a little time to
  change that here and there" shows he treated all learning as the same kind of activity —
  curiosity doesn't rank itself by prestige

## Synthesis: Feynman's decision DNA

From the 10 key events above, several core patterns in Feynman's decision-making can be
distilled:

### Pattern 1: the "reality first" principle
Runs through everything — from the Manhattan Project to the Challenger investigation, Feynman
consistently held that **what's actually true matters more than what you wish were true**. His
line "Nature cannot be fooled" isn't rhetoric — it's a conviction.

### Pattern 2: "close the option once and for all"
Choosing Caltech and never again considering another school, accepting the Nobel Prize because
declining would cause more trouble — Feynman was skilled at using one decisive choice to
eliminate a future burden of re-deciding.

### Pattern 3: "demonstration > argument"
The O-ring ice-water experiment, the Brazil teaching critique, Feynman diagrams — he proved
again and again: if you can't make someone "see" it, you haven't really solved the problem.

### Pattern 4: "curiosity has no boundary"
Drumming, lock-picking, drawing weren't "relaxation" — they were different expressions of the
same cognitive activity. Feynman refused to narrow his identity down to "physicist."

### Pattern 5: "resisting identity lock-in"
Declining honorary degrees, his wariness of the Nobel Prize, his dislike of being labelled —
Feynman consistently resisted anything trying to pin him to a fixed position.
