## 2. Core arguments that recur (3+ occurrences = a real belief)

### Argument 1: "knowing the name" is not the same as "understanding the thing"

**Frequency**: very high (runs through nearly all his writing and talks)

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* — his father's bird story | "You can know the name of that bird in all the languages of the world, but when you're finished, you'll know absolutely nothing whatever about the bird. You'll only know about humans in different places, and what they call the bird." | Primary (Feynman's own account) |
| *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* | "I learned very early the difference between knowing the name of something and knowing something." | Primary (Feynman's own account) |
| His teaching in The Feynman Lectures on Physics | Refuses jargon-stacking, insists on explaining physical concepts with analogy and intuition | Primary (teaching record) |
| The "What is Science?" address | He realized only after becoming a scientist that his father, who knew no science himself, had taught him science's fundamental principle: rote memorization is useless, you have to explore and understand | Primary |

**Distillation**: naming is a human classification label for a thing; understanding is grasping
how it actually works. The fundamental error of the education system is treating "remembering
the name" as equivalent to "mastering the knowledge." Feynman's father — a man with no
scientific background — unknowingly taught Feynman the core of scientific thinking by guiding
observation instead of feeding him vocabulary.

---

### Argument 2: don't fool yourself — and you're the easiest person to fool

**Frequency**: very high

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| "Cargo Cult Science" (1974) | "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." | Primary (the original Caltech commencement address, Caltech archive) |
| The Challenger appendix (1986) | "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for nature cannot be fooled." | Primary (an appendix to the Rogers Commission report) |
| *What Do You Care What Other People Think?* | "The only way to have real success in science is to describe the evidence very carefully without regard to the way you feel it should be." | Primary |
| *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* | Repeatedly emphasizes doubt and uncertainty as the core of the scientific spirit | Primary |

**Distillation**: this is Feynman's most central epistemological stance. Humans are naturally
prone to confirmation bias — excited by evidence that supports them, ignoring evidence that
doesn't. Feynman held that a scientist's first duty isn't discovering the truth — it's not
fooling themselves. This principle extends into his investigation of the Challenger disaster:
NASA management's failure was fundamentally self-deception (compressing the failure
probability from the engineers' 1/100 estimate to 1/100,000).

---

### Argument 3: uncertainty is not a flaw, it's strength

**Frequency**: high

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* | "I can live with doubt and uncertainty and not knowing. I think it's much more interesting to live not knowing than to have answers which might be wrong." | Primary |
| "The Value of Science" (1955) | Of science's threefold value, the third (and the highest) is "the freedom to doubt": "It is of the greatest value — the freedom to doubt." | Primary (Caltech archive) |
| "Cargo Cult Science" | Scientific integrity requires reporting every piece of evidence that could disprove your conclusion | Primary |
| *The Character of Physical Law* | Probability and uncertainty are fundamental features of the laws of physics, not measurement imprecision | Primary |

**Distillation**: Feynman distinguishes two attitudes — "I need a certain answer before I can
act" vs. "I can move forward inside the uncertainty." He held that the latter is the actual
core of the scientific spirit. This is the fundamental line between science and religion or
ideology: a scientist treats "I don't know" as a starting point, not an endpoint. Embracing
uncertainty this way isn't nihilism — it's an active cognitive strategy: it's precisely
because you admit you don't know that you have the motivation to explore.

---

### Argument 4: if you can't explain it simply, you haven't really understood it

**Frequency**: high (this is also the core of the "Feynman Technique")

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| Feynman's teaching practice | Insisted on explaining quantum mechanics in language a sixth-grader could follow | Primary |
| *QED* | Refuses to "trade distortion for surface simplicity," insisting on being accessible without misrepresenting the physics | Primary |
| *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* | The whole book is oriented around intuition and analogy — one of the most successful teaching experiments in the history of physics | Primary |

**Note**: the naming and four-step structure of the "Feynman Technique" was distilled by later
writers (mainly the Farnam Street knowledge-management community and similar) — Feynman
himself never wrote an article called "the Feynman Technique." But its core spirit —
testing your own understanding by teaching it to someone else — genuinely runs through
Feynman's teaching career.

**Reliability judgment**: the core spirit = primary; the specific "four-step" framework =
secondary (distilled by later writers)

---

### Argument 5: play and curiosity are the source of creativity

**Frequency**: high

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* — the spinning-plate story | Seeing someone throw a plate in the cafeteria and finding it fun, he started calculating the plate's rotational motion. He says doing it had "no importance whatsoever" — but this unmotivated exploration eventually led to his Nobel-winning work (Feynman diagrams) | Primary |
| *Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!* | Describes cracking safes, playing the bongos, drawing, learning Japanese — curiosity about everything is not a strategy, it's his nature | Primary |
| *The Pleasure of Finding Things Out* | The title itself is the core claim — the pleasure of exploration needs no external justification | Primary |

**Distillation**: Feynman's creativity didn't come from deliberate innovation — it came from
"if it seems fun, go study it." The spinning-plate story is his single most important
demonstration of method: when academic research started feeling stale, he decided to only do
"fun things," and that, ironically, is what led him back to the path toward the Nobel Prize.
This offers an interesting contrast to the modern "deep work" / "deliberate practice"
paradigm — Feynman's path is closer to "deep play."

---

### Argument 6: scientific integrity is the highest virtue

**Frequency**: high

| Source | Original text / key statement | Reliability |
|------|-------------|--------|
| "Cargo Cult Science" | You must report every piece of information that could prove your conclusion wrong, not just the information that supports it | Primary |
| The Challenger appendix | NASA management ignoring the O-ring data was a textbook case of scientific dishonesty | Primary |
| *What Do You Care What Other People Think?* | "In science, you learn a kind of standard integrity and honesty." | Primary |

**Distillation**: the scientific integrity Feynman defines isn't just "don't lie" — it's an
extreme form of informational transparency: actively seeking out and disclosing evidence that
could disprove your own conclusion. His performance during the Challenger investigation
(demonstrating O-ring failure on the spot with a glass of ice water) is the best practical
example of this principle in action.

## 3. Coined concepts and terminology (the skeleton of the thinking framework)

### 1. Cargo cult science

**Source**: "Cargo Cult Science" (1974), primary

**Definition**: research activity that imitates all of science's outward forms (experiments,
papers, data, peer review) but lacks the core scientific spirit (self-questioning, seeking
counter-evidence). Like Pacific islanders after World War II building fake airstrips hoping
the planes would come — the form is perfect, but it will never produce a real result.

**Scope**: far beyond science. Any activity that "did all the correct steps but lacks the core
spirit" can be called a cargo cult — cargo-cult management, cargo-cult education,
cargo-cult programming.

---

### 2. The twelve favorite problems

**Source**: MIT mathematician Gian-Carlo Rota, retelling Feynman's method (secondary, but
widely considered reliable)

**Original quote**: "You have to keep a dozen of your favorite problems constantly present in
your mind, although by and large they will lay in a dormant state. Every time you hear or read
a new trick or a new result, test it against each of your twelve problems to see whether it
helps."

**Methodological value**: this is an information filter. In an age of information overload,
Feynman's method isn't "read more" — it's "read with a question in mind." Every time you take
in a new piece of information, you collide it against the twelve questions you care about most,
and see if anything sparks. Most of the time nothing does, but occasionally you get a striking
cross-domain insight.

**Reliability**: secondary (retold by Rota), but highly consistent with Feynman's known way of
working

---

### 3. Feynman diagrams

**Source**: Feynman's physics research, primary

**Note**: a visualization tool Feynman invented for quantum electrodynamics, using simple line
segments and arrows to represent particle interactions. Though a specialist physics tool, its
underlying idea — replacing abstract formulas with visual intuition — runs through all of
Feynman's work.

---

### 4. "Nature cannot be fooled"

**Source**: the final line of the Challenger investigation appendix, primary

**Full context**: "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public
relations, for nature cannot be fooled."

**Philosophical meaning**: you can fool your boss, the public, or yourself, but you cannot fool
the laws of physics. The O-ring isn't safe just because management believed it was. This is the
ultimate expression, in engineering practice, of Feynman's "don't fool yourself" principle.

## 4. The threefold value of science (The Value of Science, 1955)

This talk deserves its own section, because it's Feynman's most systematic statement of value
theory:

| Level of value | Content | Feynman's attitude |
|---------|------|-----------|
| First | Scientific knowledge lets us do things, build things (instrumental value) | Acknowledges it, but doesn't think it's the most important |
| Second | Science stimulates the imagination — the intellectual pleasure of reading, writing, and researching | Values it |
| **Third** | **The freedom to doubt — it's okay to admit "I don't know"** | **Considers this the highest value** |

Feynman's original text emphasizes: scientific knowledge is a set of "statements with varying
degrees of certainty" — some nearly certain, some probably correct, some completely unknown.
Being able to sit comfortably with that uncertainty is the greatest gift science gives
humanity.

**Source reliability**: primary (the original text from the December 1955 issue of Caltech's
Engineering and Science magazine, archived)

## 5. Intellectual lineage — Feynman's "upstream" and "downstream"

### Upstream (people and ideas that influenced Feynman)

| Person/source | How they influenced him | Reliability |
|----------|---------|--------|
| His father, Melville Feynman | Taught him to "observe rather than name," even though his father wasn't a scientist himself | Primary (Feynman recounts this many times) |
| His wife, Arline Greenbaum | The line "why do you care what other people think" shaped Feynman's independent-mindedness. Arline encouraged him not to care what others thought of him | Primary |
| John Archibald Wheeler | Feynman's advisor at Princeton, co-developed the path-integral method with him | Primary (academic record) |
| Paul Dirac | Feynman diagrams were partly inspired by Dirac's formalism of quantum mechanics | Primary (academic lineage) |
| The Encyclopaedia Britannica | Feynman's self-study material as a teenager, through which he built his own knowledge system | Primary (mentioned in the autobiography) |

### Downstream (fields and people Feynman influenced)

| Field/person | How they were influenced |
|----------|---------|
| Nanotechnology | Feynman's 1959 talk "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom" is considered the intellectual origin of nanotechnology |
| Quantum computing | Feynman's 1981 proposal to simulate a quantum system with another quantum system makes him one of the pioneers of quantum computing |
| Farnam Street / Shane Parrish | Systematized the Feynman Technique into a mental-models framework, influencing Silicon Valley's knowledge-management culture |
| Tiago Forte / Building a Second Brain | The "twelve favorite problems" was adopted as a core tool in his knowledge-management system |
| First-principles thinking | Elon Musk has repeatedly cited a Feynman-style method of rederiving from fundamentals |

## 6. Summary of Feynman's core mental models

| Model | Core question | How to apply it |
|------|---------|---------|
| Naming ≠ understanding | Do I actually understand this, or did I just memorize the name? | Try explaining it in words a sixth-grader could follow |
| Don't fool yourself | Am I selectively looking at the evidence? | Actively seek out the counter-evidence |
| Cargo-cult detection | Does this practice have form, or substance? | Check whether it's only imitating the outward form |
| The twelve-question filter | Is this new piece of information relevant to what I care about most? | Collide every new piece of information against the twelve questions |
| Embracing uncertainty | Is "I don't know" an endpoint or a starting point? | Treat uncertainty as an invitation to explore |
| Deep play | Can something fun lead somewhere profound? | Follow curiosity, don't only do "important things" |
| Nature cannot be fooled | Does reality actually match my expectations? | Respect the empirical data, don't self-deceive for PR |
